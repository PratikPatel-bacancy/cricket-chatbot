# No-Balls and Wides

## No-ball: what causes it

A delivery is called a no-ball by the umpire for several reasons, the most common being:

- **Front-foot no-ball**: The bowler's front foot lands with no part behind the popping crease (the line level with the stumps at the batting end) at the moment of delivery. Some part of the front foot must land behind (on the bowler's side of) this line.
- **Back-foot no-ball**: In the bowler's delivery stride, the back foot must land within and not touching the return crease (the side lines of the bowling area).
- **High full toss ("beamer")**: A ball that passes, without bouncing, above waist height of the striker standing upright at the crease is a no-ball (and if bowled dangerously/deliberately, can also result in the bowler being removed from the attack).
- **Above shoulder height (fast bowlers, short deliveries)**: A ball that passes above the striker's shoulder height without bouncing is also a no-ball.
- **Bowling from the wrong place**: If the bowler's back foot lands outside the return crease, or the ball is thrown rather than bowled with a straight arm (an illegal action / "chucking" called separately as a no-ball), it is called.
- **Fielding-side violations**: More than two fielders behind square on the leg side at the point of delivery, or a fielder standing in dangerous/unfair positions, can also result in a no-ball.

## Consequences of a no-ball

- One extra run is added to the batting team's total (in most formats).
- The delivery does not count as one of the six balls of the over — it must be re-bowled.
- The batter cannot be given out in most standard dismissal modes off a no-ball — specifically, a batter CANNOT be out bowled, caught, LBW, stumped, or hit wicket off a no-ball. However, the batter CAN still be out run out, or out for handling the ball / obstructing the field / hitting the ball twice, even off a no-ball.
- In limited-overs cricket, a no-ball is often followed by a "free hit" — the next delivery, the batter cannot be dismissed by any method except run out (rules vary slightly by competition).

## Wide: what causes it

A delivery is called wide if, in the umpire's judgement, the ball passes so far from the striker (too high, too wide down leg side, or too wide outside off stump) that the batter could not reasonably hit it with a normal cricket shot from a standard guard position. Modern limited-overs cricket applies wide guidelines relative to lines marked on the pitch for the leg side and off side.

## Consequences of a wide

- One extra run is added to the batting team's total.
- The delivery does not count as one of the six balls of the over — it must be re-bowled.
- Batters can still run additional runs off a wide (e.g. byes past the wicketkeeper), which are added on top of the single wide run.
- A batter can be out off a wide only by being run out, stumped, hitting the ball twice, handling the ball, or obstructing the field — not bowled, caught, LBW, or hit wicket.
